# Run protocol

## Synthetic greywater

Stock, 5 litres. Enough for a calibration series and three column charges.

| Component | Quantity | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tap water | 5 L | — |
| Fine soil, through the finest sieve to hand | 15 g | suspended solids |
| Neutral liquid soap | 5 ml | surfactant load |
| Blue food colouring | 10 drops | adsorption tracer |

Mix, then stir again immediately before drawing every sample. Solids settle in minutes and a settled stock reads as clean water.

**Check before going further:** with the jar full of undiluted stock, the target card behind it must still be faintly visible. If the target disappears completely the contrast measurement saturates and the calibration is worthless. Too opaque, add water; too clear, add soil in 5 g steps.

Write down whatever you end up with. The absolute values do not matter, repeatability across the session does.

## Before the first run

1. Grade the scoria. Keep a fine and a coarse fraction, measure the volume of
   both, record the yield from the raw scoria. See [grading](#grading-the-scoria) below.
2. Prepare the charcoal and leave it to soak overnight. See
   [preparing the charcoal](#preparing-the-charcoal) below.
3. Load the column bottom up per RWL-001. Level each layer, insect mesh disc between layers.
4. Wash the bed with clean water until the effluent runs clear. New media sheds fines and will otherwise read as if the column is making water dirtier.
5. Build the rig per RWL-002. Take one frame, read the target and white ROIs off it, write `setup.json`.

## Grading the scoria

Laboratory sieves give the cleanest result, but they are not required. Whatever method is used, report it plainly and give the measured numbers rather than a nominal mesh size.

**With laboratory sieves.** Standard stack, keep the 0.4–0.8 mm and 4–8 mm fractions, report D10 and the uniformity coefficient.

**With kitchen sieves.** Use three sieves of different fineness. Measure the aperture of each by counting wires per centimetre under good light and dividing, then report the fractions by that measured aperture. Less precise, still traceable to a number you actually measured.

**By sedimentation.** No equipment at all. Crush the scoria, put it in a tall transparent bottle of water, shake, and let it settle. Coarse grains reach the bottom first and fines last, so decanting at timed intervals separates the fractions. This is settling-velocity separation, the same principle as the hydrometer analysis used in soil mechanics.

Record the settling times and the mass recovered in each fraction. Two runs of the same procedure should give similar masses; if they do not, the crushing was inconsistent.

## Preparing the charcoal

Lump charcoal, never briquettes: briquettes carry binders that leach into the
water. Pieces should look like broken black wood, not uniform pillows.

1. Crush it inside two nested plastic bags. Pressure works better than blows;
   stand on a board laid over the bag, or roll a glass bottle across it.
2. Pass it through the insect mesh and **keep what stays on top**. The powder
   that falls through would wash out and turn the effluent black.
3. Rinse in a bucket, changing the water, until it only runs faintly grey.
4. Soak overnight until it sinks. **Dry charcoal floats**: it is full of air and
   will rise through the layer above it if loaded dry. Simmering it in water
   for 15 minutes does the same job faster.

Charcoal that cannot be crushed evenly still works as a graded layer: coarse
pieces at the bottom, fine on top, so the water meets the fine material first.

Do not pour wash water down a drain. Let it settle, pour off the clear water,
and put the sediment in the bin; scoria fines and charcoal dust block pipes.

## Target sheet

The checker target and white reference card for RWL-002 are generated by the
toolchain at true size:

```bash
rwl target --out target-sheet.pdf
```

A ready copy is at [print/target-sheet.pdf](print/target-sheet.pdf). Print at
100% and check the 100 mm scale bar with a ruler before cutting.

## Calibration series

Five levels, 200 ml each, measured with a kitchen measuring jug and syringes.

| Sample | Stock | Clean water |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `cal0` | 0 ml | 200 ml |
| `cal1` | 50 ml | 150 ml |
| `cal2` | 100 ml | 100 ml |
| `cal3` | 150 ml | 50 ml |
| `cal4` | 200 ml | 0 ml |

Photograph each in the same jar, washing and drying it between levels. Check the regions on the first frame, then measure:

```bash
rwl roi photos/cal0.jpg --setup setup.json
rwl measure photos/cal*.jpg --setup setup.json --out readings.csv
rwl calibrate --readings readings.csv --levels levels.csv --out calibration.json
```

R² below 0.9 means the light drifted. Reshoot the series; do not continue.

## The run

1. Stir the stock. Fill the jar, photograph it as `influent`.
2. Close the valve. Pour 750 ml of stock into the column, the 200 mm charge of RWL-001.
3. Open the valve and start the timer.
4. Collect the effluent in the measuring jug. Record the time to pass 500 ml — that is the flow rate.
5. Fill the jar from the effluent, photograph as `charge1`.
6. Repeat steps 2–5 for `charge2` and `charge3`, stirring the stock each time.
7. Process:

```bash
rwl measure photos/influent.jpg photos/charge*.jpg --setup setup.json --out run.csv
rwl report --readings run.csv --calibration calibration.json \
    --before influent --after charge1 charge2 charge3
```

The first charge will read best and then removal drops as the bed loads; that curve is the useful result, not the single first number. `report` prints it as one row per charge.

## Hydraulic conductivity

Step 4 is a falling-head permeability test: the column is its own standpipe. Measure once, with a tape, how far the hose outlet sits below the base of the bed, then run the first charge with the valve fully open:

```bash
rwl permeability --seconds 45 --outlet-drop 0.30
```

It prints the bed's hydraulic conductivity, the same bed at 0.37 g, and how long 500 ml should take at slow sand filtration rates. If the column runs faster than that, throttle the valve for the measured runs.

For a full drainage curve instead of one time, float a cork with a skewer standing up out of the pipe, film it against a ruler, and read the level off the video every few seconds into a CSV of `seconds,level_m` (water above the bed):

```bash
rwl permeability --series level.csv --outlet-drop 0.30
```

## What to record

Written down at the time, not reconstructed later.

- Depth and volume of each medium as placed, and the graded yield from the raw scoria
- The grading method and its measured aperture or settling times
- The greywater recipe actually used
- Flow rate per charge, and the outlet drop below the bed
- Removal percentage per charge
- Room temperature

## Photographs to take anyway

Independent of the measurement, these are needed for the project record.

- Every stage of the build, including the versions that did not work
- The graded fractions side by side after sieving
- The five calibration jars lined up as a gradient
- Influent and effluent jars side by side
- The column running, from the side, with the layers visible
